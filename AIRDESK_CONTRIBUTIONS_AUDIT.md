## A. ROUTING AND JURISDICTION

A1. Law applies to the departure country. Not the airline's nationality. Not the
destination.
→ IN: Module 1, Step 1 and Step 2 headers.

A2. A non-EU carrier departing from an EU airport is covered by EU261.
Emirates flying London to Dubai is not an EU departure. EU261 does not apply.
→ IN: Module 1 edge cases.

A3. EU departure via a non-EU hub (e.g. London → Dubai → Sydney): EU261 applies
to the EU departure segment only. It does not extend to the Dubai → Sydney leg
even when it is on the same ticket.
→ IN: Module 1 edge cases.

A4. Codeshare: law applies to departure country regardless. At the airport, the
operating carrier handles duty of care. For compensation, go to the marketing
(ticketing) carrier first. If redirected, quote IATA 735d.
→ IN: Module 1 edge cases.

A5. Own airline staff strikes are eligible for compensation. This includes pilots,
cabin crew, flight engineers, and any internal staff. The ECJ Krüsemann ruling
(2018) confirmed wildcat strikes by an airline's own staff are not extraordinary
circumstances.
→ IN: Module 1, Step 3.

A6. Airport or border security strikes, ATC strikes, and baggage handler strikes
where those handlers are a third party — these are not the airline's
responsibility and can be valid extraordinary circumstances.
→ IN: Module 1, Step 3.

A7. Compensation, refund, and reimbursement are three separate entitlements that
can apply simultaneously. Airlines present them as either/or. They are not.
→ IN: Module 1, Step 4 and restated in Modules 2A, 2B, 2C.

A8. Crew strikes in the EU are now compensable following 2026 EU updates.
→ IN: Module 1, EU departure entry.

## B. IATA RESOLUTIONS AND INTERLINE MECHANICS

B1. IATA Resolution 735d: involuntary disruptions are the airline's obligation
regardless of booking channel. An OTA cannot redirect a passenger to the airline
for an airline-caused disruption. Applies on day of travel and day prior to
IATA member carriers party to MITA.
→ IN: Module 4, core rule.

B2. INVOL endorsement: must appear in the first five characters of the
endorsement/restriction field on any replacement ticket issued under 735d.
Without it, the new operating carrier has grounds to refuse the ticket at check-in.
→ IN: Modules 2A and 4.

B3. 2-day rule under 735d: a reissued ticket is recognised as involuntary only
if issued within 2 days of the first scheduled departure.
→ IN: Module 4.

B4. 5-day rule under 735d: the INVOL designation is invalid if departure is 5 or
more days from the reissue date.
→ IN: Module 4.

B5. Alliance rerouting under 735d: the rerouting obligation extends to alliance
partner carriers. The passenger can explicitly request partner inventory under
Star Alliance, Oneworld, or SkyTeam protocols.
→ IN: Modules 2A and 4.

B6. 72-hour operating carrier takeover: within 72 hours of departure, if the
marketing carrier is not providing rebooking options, the operating carrier has
authority under 735d to take over the ticket directly.
→ IN: Module 4.

B7. IATA Resolution 766: the delivering carrier — the airline that brought the
passenger to the connection point late — is responsible for cancelling unused
onward space and rebooking. The onward carrier's desk is not the right first stop.
→ IN: Modules 2B, 2D, and 4.

B8. IATA Resolution 737 (Refunds): when any segment on a multi-carrier ticket is
cancelled, full ticket refund responsibility sits with the marketing carrier. The
interline billing recovery between carriers is the marketing carrier's problem.
Not the passenger's and not the OTA's.
→ IN: Module 12 Foundation.

B9. When OTA says the airline cannot process because of interline complexity:
the OTA is accepting an invalid reason without pushing back. Airlines sometimes
deny the interline call for codeshare non-alliance partners simply because it is
operationally effortful — they have to find the right number, queue, and get
authorisation. That is operational avoidance, not a valid reason to deny. The OTA
must escalate to the airline's interline desk, not the counter and not the call
centre.
→ IN: Module 12 Foundation (interline call hierarchy) and Module 4 (OTA
assertiveness requirement).

B10. Full ticket refund on multi-carrier cancellation: it generally triggers a
full refund. The responsibility stays with the marketing carrier.
→ IN: Module 12 Foundation.

## C. OTA MECHANICS

C1. OTA waiver code unlock: when an airline disrupts a flight, it issues internal
waiver codes to OTAs allowing fee-free changes and refunds outside normal fare
rules. Asking the OTA "Has the airline issued a waiver code for this disruption?"
surprises agents, signals the passenger knows the process, and shifts the
conversation. Raises resolution odds roughly 40%.
→ IN: Module 4.

C2. OTA escalation chain as it actually runs: passenger calls OTA → OTA calls
operating carrier → if denied, OTA calls marketing carrier → marketing carrier
contacts alliance desk → alliance desk reaches validating carrier. Every step is
a separate call, queue, and transfer. This is why calls take hours.
→ IN: Module 4.

C3. Do not hang up and call back with the OTA. The agent who started the
escalation chain owns that thread. Starting again means going back to zero.
→ IN: Module 4.

C4. First three digits of the ticket number identify the validating carrier —
the carrier that holds the money and has ticketing authority. Passengers have
this document and do not know how to read it.
→ IN: Module 4.

C5. If the airline has issued a directive or waiver, the OTA will act on it.
When the OTA says it cannot help, the real blocker is that the airline has not
yet issued direction. That is the only actual blocker. Airlines rarely formally
deny involuntary rebooking on record.
→ IN: Module 4 (updated logic section).

C6. OTA operational errors create OTA liability — failing to pass a schedule
change notification from the airline, processing voluntary as involuntary or
vice versa, failing to apply a waiver code already issued. OTAs cover their own
errors but only when identified and pushed. They do not volunteer it.
→ IN: Module 4, Step 4.

C7. When an airline touches an OTA booking at the counter, the OTA loses
synchronisation. The OTA system no longer matches what the airline changed.
Future servicing of remaining legs becomes complicated. Airlines therefore
typically help only for one leg or one-way, rarely legs 2–4 of a round trip.
After accepting airline help at the counter: immediately brief the OTA in
writing on what was changed.
→ IN: Module 2D (OTA booking problem at counter section).

## D. BAGGAGE

D1. PIR must be filed before leaving the baggage hall. Filing after — even hours
later — kills most claims. If the desk is unstaffed: photograph it with a
timestamp, note the time, call the airline immediately from the airport.
The attempt is evidence even if it cannot be filed in person.
→ IN: Module 5, first section.

D2. PIR reference number format: [airport code][airline code][digits], e.g.
LHRBA12345. The airport code identifies where the bag was last handled. The
airline code identifies the responsible carrier.
→ IN: Module 5.

D3. Last handling carrier rule: the airline that last handled the bag is
responsible for the claim, regardless of who sold the ticket or who operated
the first flight.
→ IN: Module 5.

D4. WorldTracer status codes with plain-language meanings:
SHL — Short Landed: bag expected on flight but did not arrive.
AHL — After Landing: bag found at a station with no matching owner.
TRC — Tracing: active search in progress.
FWD — Forward: bag located and being sent.
OHD — On Hand: bag physically at a location, ready for delivery or pickup.
DEL — Delivered: bag delivered.
DPR — Damage Property Report: damage report filed.
→ IN: Module 5.

D5. wheresmysuitcase.com: delivery tracking once the airline confirms the bag is
dispatched. Can hold delivery, change address, waive signature. Damage at
delivery must be reported within 24 hours. Participating carriers include AA,
BA, AC, DL, UA, LH, EK, AF, WS and 12+ others.
→ IN: Module 5.

D6. Montreal Convention updated 28 December 2024: baggage liability is now
1,519 SDR per passenger, approximately USD $2,000 / CAD $2,858 / £1,600. Any
airline quoting 1,288 SDR is using the outdated figure. Correct them explicitly.
→ IN: Modules 1 and 5.

D7. Air Canada specifically: their Customer Service Plan still shows 1,288 SDR
as of the knowledge base build date. Must be corrected on any Canadian bag claim.
→ IN: Module 5.

D8. Baggage allowance on involuntary rerouting: under Resolution 735d Article 4,
the passenger is entitled to the baggage allowance of the original itinerary on
the rerouted journey. If rerouted from Business to Economy, the Business allowance
still applies. Most passengers do not know this and accept Economy restrictions.
→ IN: Module 5.

D9. Three prevention steps before checking any bag: (1) remove all old bag tags
because old barcodes misroute bags; (2) photograph bag contents before closing —
two minutes and vital for claims; (3) put a paper with name and contact number
inside the bag as a backup if the external tag is lost.
→ IN: Module 5.

D10. Through check-in: single ticket, one booking reference — bags go to final
destination (with exception for US, Canada, Australia which may require customs
re-check). Separate tickets — bags stop at the first destination. Passenger must
collect, clear customs if required, and re-check. This is a separate risk and
needs significant time allowance.
→ IN: Module 5.

D11. Bag fee structure: cheapest at time of booking, more expensive online after
booking, substantially higher at check-in desk, highest at the gate. For budget
carriers the gap is largest — a £10 online bag can cost £50+ at the gate.
→ IN: Module 5.

D12. Cabin bag dimension trap: measure including handles extended and wheels
included. One centimetre over the sizer triggers gate check at the highest fee.
→ IN: Module 5.

## E. FARE BASIS CODES AND MULTI-CARRIER TICKETS

E1. Fare basis code is the alphanumeric string attached to each flight coupon. It
governs the specific fare rules for that coupon: change fee, refund value, baggage
allowance, flexibility, restrictions.
→ IN: Module 12 Foundation.

E2. If all coupons on a ticket have the same fare basis code, one set of rules
applies throughout. Simplest ticket to service.
→ IN: Module 12 Foundation.

E3. If the outbound and inbound portions have different fare basis codes, they
are governed by different rules. The change fee, refund, and baggage on the
outbound do not equal those on the inbound. Agents who quote a flat fee for
all legs on split-fare tickets are giving wrong information. Refunds must be
calculated per portion, not as one figure.
→ IN: Module 12 Foundation.

E4. Multi-carrier tickets: each carrier applies its own fare rules to its own
coupons. Each carrier applies its own baggage rules at check-in. Baggage
allowance is governed by the ticketing carrier's rules unless a through-check
agreement exists between the carriers. If no through-check, passenger collects
bags at each carrier's last point. Verify baggage allowance with each operating
carrier separately.
→ IN: Modules 12 Foundation and 5.

E5. Multi-carrier TIMATIC check: the first airline's clearance at departure does
not bind the second airline at the connection point. Each carrier performs its
own independent TIMATIC check with its own interpretation of entry, transit, and
health requirements. A passenger may be denied at the second leg despite passing
the first check.
→ IN: Module 12 Foundation.

E6. Name corrections cost more on split-fare tickets because each coupon is a
separate fare calculation. A correction on a ZNCZA coupon has different economics
than on an ANCZA coupon even on the same ticket.
→ PARTIAL (re-accounted v3.2): Module 12 Foundation EXISTS in KB v3.2 and
covers fare basis codes, same/different fare-basis rules, and split-fare /
multi-carrier pricing (E1–E5 are reflected there, with the ZNCZA/ANCZA
examples). The KB still flags the full Module 12 as "to be built separately",
and this specific point — that name-correction COST differs by fare basis on
split-fare tickets — is not yet captured. Residual gap narrowed to the
name-correction cost economics only.

## F. AIRLINE-SPECIFIC BEHAVIOUR

F1. BA: the most legally compliant major carrier. If the regulation entitles a
passenger to something, BA will pay it precisely. If it does not, they will not
give it — no goodwill, no extras. They account for minutes in delay calculations
and cents in fare differences.
→ IN: Module 3.

F2. BA handles interline calls: they call partners and receive calls. This is
one of the few carriers that makes the interline call without being pushed. For
OTA bookings with BA as marketing carrier, BA engages the interline process
correctly.
→ IN: Module 3.

F3. US carriers (AA, Delta, United, Southwest): gate agents have real authority
that contact centre agents do not. The discretionary flexibility is higher than
European carriers. The airport is always a better option than the phone. US
agents say yes to goodwill requests more often than European agents.
→ IN: Module 3.

F4. AA specifics from their Conditions of Carriage and International Tariff: 40%
downgrade refund on the affected segment. Flight number change alone triggers
involuntary refund right even if times are identical. Schedule change within 72
hours of departure: 90-minute change triggers involuntary refund right. Force
majeure wording in the CoC is broader than in the International Tariff; the
tariff governs internationally.
→ IN: Modules 3 and 2E.

F5. Agents at airlines and OTAs sometimes misinterpret fare rules and pass
incorrect information to customers. Fare rules and ticketing conditions vary by
airline and are hard to structure into universal help articles. On Amadeus, scripts
sometimes add unnecessary entries to the PNR, making it longer and harder to
service on automation.
→ MISSING from KB (still, v3.2): operational context explaining why agents give
wrong information — not captured as a formal passenger advisory or framing note
anywhere in the KB. A one-paragraph framing note in Module 12 or Module 3
remains the fix.

## I. OPERATIONAL INTELLIGENCE (TACTICAL MOVES)

I1. Extraordinary circumstances challenge tactic: ask the airline for written
documentation of exactly what caused the specific flight to be cancelled, which
extraordinary circumstance applies, and when operations first became aware. Most
airlines cannot produce specific documentation for crew shortages or routine
technical faults. Raises resolution odds roughly 30%.
→ IN: Module 2A.

I2. Alliance inventory unlock tactic: explicitly request partner airline inventory
by name (Star Alliance / Oneworld / SkyTeam) under IATA 735d. Agents often do
not show this inventory unless asked. Raises chance of better routing roughly 50%.
→ IN: Module 2A.

I3. Arrival delay rule: compensation and rights are calculated on arrival delay
at the final destination, not departure delay of any single flight. A flight that
departs four hours late but arrives 2.5 hours late does not trigger EU261 threshold.
→ IN: Module 2B (with ECJ case citations).

I4. MCT 5-minute rule: if an inbound delay leaves less than the published minimum
connection time before the onward departure, the airline is responsible. Five
minutes below MCT is a valid claim. No tolerance and no grey zone.
→ IN: Module 2B and 2D.

I5. MCT booked below minimum from the start: this is a stronger claim than a
delay-caused missed connection. The original booking itself was an airline
booking error. The passenger can request involuntary rerouting at no cost on
that basis.
→ IN: Module 2D.

I6. Voluntary/involuntary misclassification: airline-caused disruptions must be
processed as involuntary under IATA Resolution 737 — full amount plus all taxes,
no penalty. If the airline processes it as voluntary, a penalty is deducted that
should not apply. This is one of the most common refund errors.
→ IN: Modules 2A and 2F.

I7. Duty of care is owed even in extraordinary circumstances. Meals, hotel, and
transport are always owed. This is the most commonly misrepresented right in
aviation. Airlines do not offer proactively. Passengers who ask get it.
→ IN: Modules 2A, 2B, and all disruption modules.

I8. Cancellation certificate: must be obtained before discussing alternatives, before
signing anything, before accepting a rerouting offer. Without this document all
subsequent claims are weaker.
→ IN: Module 2A.

I9. Automatic rebooking rejection window: the window closes when the new flight
departs. If the automatic rerouting is not acceptable, the passenger must reject
it before that flight leaves.
→ IN: Module 2A, Move 2.

I10. Goodwill gesture trap: before signing any form, ask "does accepting this
affect any future compensation claim I might have?" If the agent cannot confirm
it does not, do not sign.
→ IN: Module 2A (Trap 1).

I11. Refund trap: before accepting a refund, check the current market price of
a replacement route. Rerouting means the airline bears the price difference.
Refund means the passenger bears it. Replacement often costs three to five times
the original.
→ IN: Modules 1, 2A (Trap 2).

I12. Verbal agreements trap: any arrangement must be confirmed in writing before
leaving the desk. If it is not in writing, it did not happen.
→ IN: Module 2A (Trap 3).

I13. Separate ticket trap: different booking references mean two separate
contracts. If a delay on the first ticket causes the second to be missed, the
second has no obligation to accommodate. Rebook at current market price, often
three to five times the original. Preventive rule: four to six hours minimum
between separate tickets; overnight buffer on low-frequency routes.
→ IN: Modules 2B, 2D, and 4.

I14. Airport authority gap: a duty manager at the airport has authority a contact
centre agent does not have. Urgency creates solutions that phone calls cannot.
US carriers show the largest authority gap. For any immediate resolution, get
to the airport desk.
→ IN: Modules 2B and 3.

I15. Schedule change credit types: Use By (all travel must complete within one
year of original booking date), Commence By (travel must start within one year),
and Complete Travel By (all legs must finish within one year). These look similar
but have different expiry mechanics. Get the type in writing before accepting.
→ IN: Module 2E.

I16. Journey rendered useless: a schedule change that arrives on time but makes
the purpose of travel impossible — event finished, cruise departed, hotel window
missed — is grounds for a full refund. Strongest when the purpose was time-sensitive
and previously documented.
→ IN: Module 2E.

I17. Letter before action as settlement trigger: most airlines settle before MCOL
is actually filed. The filing triggers the airline's legal team, not customer
service. UK small claims fee is under £35 for claims up to £1,000.
→ IN: Module 2F.

I18. FlightAware and FlightRadar24: independent sources airlines cannot dispute.
Screenshot showing actual departure and arrival times. Screenshot immediately —
data is archived but access is easier fresh.
→ IN: Module 2F.

I19. DOT denied boarding — US domestic: compensation is cash or check, payable
at the airport, same day. The airline requires the passenger's written agreement
to substitute a voucher. Without written agreement, cash is required.
→ IN: Modules 1 and 2C.
→ NOTE (resolved in v3.2): Module 1 and Module 2C now both use $1,075 (1–2hr) /
$2,150 (2hr+) domestic and $1,075 (1–4hr) / $2,150 (4hr+) international. The
pre-2024 $775 / $1,550 figures are gone; the v3.2 changelog confirms Module 1
source-of-truth figures govern. No remaining inconsistency.

I20. Downgrade: not the same as denied boarding, but both rights may apply
simultaneously. EU261: 25% / 50% / 75% reimbursement depending on flight length.
AA specifically: 40% of ticketed fare on the affected segment per their tariff.
→ IN: Modules 2C and 3.

I21. Compensation for missed connection always calculated to the final
destination. London → Amsterdam (missed) → Singapore, arriving six hours late
in Singapore: compensation is €600 (London–Singapore distance), not €250
(London–Amsterdam only). This is consistently underclaimed.
→ IN: Modules 2C and 2D.
